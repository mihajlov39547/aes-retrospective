CPU/GPU AES ENCRYPTION ON ACCESSIBLE COMPUTING PLATFORMS: PERFORMANCE AND CONDITIONAL COST EFFICIENCY\par}
\end{center}
\vspace{.2cm}
\section*{Abstract}
This paper presents validated AES-128-CTR experiments and derives conditional resource-cost thresholds. A study on a Windows x64 machine with an Intel i5-10300H and GTX 1650 Ti compares a single-stream CPU AES-NI transform, a resident CUDA kernel, and a prepared GPU pipeline. The CPU outperforms the pipeline at 1, 16, and 32 MiB. At 32 MiB, their rates are 1455.8, 4800.6, and 1204.5 MB/s for CPU, GPU kernel, and pipeline, respectively. Equal attributable hourly charges favor the CPU's modeled cost per GB; the pipeline's GPU-to-CPU hourly-price threshold is 0.8273. A separate Linux/T4 campaign uses CPU OpenSSL EVP and independent 1 MiB pages. Increasing the batch from one to 32 pages raises the median of session CPU-to-GPU time ratios from 0.5404 to 0.9125, but the CPU transform remains faster throughout the tested range. The campaigns are not pooled because implementations and timing boundaries differ. Resident results indicate potential when transfers are unnecessary; Parquet is a motivating example, not a benchmarked application. The contribution is a conditional decision framework, not observed monetary savings or a universal procurement recommendation.

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} AES-CTR; AES-NI; CUDA; CPU/GPU; performance evaluation; conditional cost efficiency; business information systems

\section{Introduction}
Data protection in business information systems requires cryptographically correct implementations as well as efficient use of existing processing and memory infrastructure. Encryption lies on the path between data processing and storage; its duration can affect system throughput, resource occupancy, and capacity planning. Introducing GPU acceleration is not merely a technical issue: dedicated infrastructure entails fixed costs, while resource occupancy and data transfers can limit the achievable benefit.\par
The AES standard defines the cryptographic transformation \citep{nist_aes2023}, while CTR mode enables independent blocks to be processed \citep{nist_ctr2001}. Modern CPUs can use dedicated AES-NI support \citep{intel_aesni}, whereas GPUs parallelize block operations across large numbers of threads. However, high isolated GPU kernel throughput does not demonstrate a shorter total processing time when the input data reside in system memory.\par
Based on the experiments conducted, this paper aims to (i) distinguish the technical timing boundaries of CPU/GPU measurements, (ii) compare throughput on accessible platforms, and (iii) establish the condition for a lower attributable cost per GB implied by those measurements. The central research question is: \emph{at what price ratio for occupied CPU and GPU resources can the technical result change the choice of implementation path?} This connects the technical investigation with the economics of IT infrastructure use, without claiming actual financial savings.\par
The contribution is an experimental comparison of validated CPU/GPU execution paths with explicitly defined timing boundaries, complemented by a compute-time cost model. The measurements provide the technical basis for deriving conditional thresholds for the relative price of processing services. Performance was measured experimentally, whereas cost indicators were derived from those measurements and the stated model assumptions; actual market savings were not measured.

\section{Literature Review and Business Application Context}
Studies of GPU AES implementations show that memory organization and parallelization can substantially change kernel throughput \citep{iwai2012,tezcan2021}. Nevertheless, \citet{gregg2011} emphasize that data location and transfers between CPU and GPU memory determine the meaning of the comparison itself. \citet{lee2010} likewise point out that uneven optimization of CPU and GPU baselines can produce misleading speedup factors. Accordingly, the primary CPU baseline in this paper is not limited to software-only AES but includes an explicit AES-NI path.\par
The energy aspects of heterogeneous AES computing have already been addressed in the literature. \citet{fei2020} investigate the energy efficiency of parallel AES through workload distribution, overlap, and frequency selection, while \citet{barroso2007} discuss the principles of energy-proportional computing. Unlike energy-focused experiments, the present study did not collect validated data on joules per GB. The economic analysis therefore considers only \emph{modeled costs of occupied compute time}, rather than energy consumption or total cost of ownership.\par
A concrete application example is modular encryption of Apache Parquet columnar files. The \texttt{AES\_GCM\_CTR\_V1} specification uses AES-CTR for data pages, while other modules are protected using AES-GCM; integrity protection for the CTR pages themselves is not provided in the same way as in full GCM mode \citep{parquet_encrypt}. Organizing multiple pages raises the question of batch processing and the cost of moving data between devices. In the observed GTX study, the resident GPU kernel at 32 MiB has 3.30 times the throughput of CPU AES-NI, indicating the GPU's computational potential for batches of pages already resident on the device, but without a demonstrated advantage for the complete pipeline. The supplementary T4 experiment with 32 independent pages shows the opposite outcome for the prepared pipeline: a CPU/GPU time ratio of 0.9125. Thus, Parquet is a \emph{conditional candidate} for GPU offloading, not a demonstrated financial saving. This is a \emph{motivating example}, not a benchmark result for a Parquet system: our synthetic buffers are not Parquet files and do not include formatting, compression, metadata, storage, or key management. The Parquet specification does not define a market price for encryption per GB; that price depends on the specific execution environment.\par
The research contribution is neither a new cryptographic algorithm nor a universal TCO model, but a connection between documented timing boundaries and a conditional indicator of compute-time cost. Related technical and energy studies already exist; a claim of complete methodological novelty would require an additional systematic review.

\section{Methodology and Timing Boundaries}
\subsection{Primary Platform and Validation}
The primary platform is a Windows 10 x64 machine with consumer hardware: an Intel Core i5-10300H (4 physical / 8 logical cores), 16 GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA GTX 1650 Ti (4 GB). AES-128-CTR was tested using PyCryptodome 3.23.0 with an explicitly selected native AES-NI path, alongside a CUDA baseline and a simple optimization variant, v1. Known-answer vector validation, verification of the selected native backend, and paired identical inputs preceded measurement. Five study sessions with seeds 2003--2007 use five warm-up iterations and twenty retained repetitions per group. A total of 6000 observations were retained for the broader ECB/CTR dataset; the economic model uses the CTR records.\par
The CPU path is sequential at the application level: one synchronous \texttt{encrypt()} call per observation. Neither the processor's maximum multithreaded capacity nor controlled physical-core affinity was measured; therefore, \emph{single-stream CPU AES-NI transform} is more precise than a claim covering all physical aspects of single-threaded execution.\par
CPU transform timing includes output allocation and the library call but excludes cipher construction and key scheduling. The resident GPU result measures an isolated kernel operating on data already prepared in device memory. The GPU pipeline includes the H2D transfer, kernel, D2H transfer, and synchronization, but excludes device and pinned-memory allocations, prior copying into a pinned buffer, key preparation, storage I/O, and business-process orchestration \citep{nvidia_guide}. This asymmetry means that the CPU transform and GPU pipeline are \emph{useful for a conditional comparison}, but do not cover identical boundaries of a complete business process.\par
Throughput in decimal MB/s is calculated as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rate}R=\frac{N}{10^6T},\end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of bytes and $T$ is the measured duration in seconds. The primary reported statistic is the median of five session throughput medians. Paired time ratios are calculated separately as the median of five ratios of session median durations; they need not be numerically equal to the ratio of aggregated throughputs.

\subsection{Tesla T4 and Additional Accessible Resources}
The supplementary experimental component includes methodologically separate measurements on an NVIDIA Tesla T4 and its CPU host in a Linux environment, as well as Windows/ARM CPU validations. The T4 is an accelerator that can be accessed in certain free or limited cloud research environments, including Google Colab when available; this does not make it a consumer graphics card or guarantee free and continuous availability \citep{colab}. Differences in processors, libraries, environments, and timing boundaries preclude pooling these samples with the primary GTX study.\par
A separate T4 experiment processes 1, 4, 16, and 32 independent 1 MiB pages in AES-128-CTR mode. It uses an Intel Xeon host running Linux and OpenSSL 3.0.13 EVP, unlike the Windows/PyCryptodome AES-NI path of the primary GTX campaign. The CPU baseline measures a prepared native EVP transform with preallocated output, while the GPU baseline measures a prepared pipeline. For each batch size, the ratio of the session median CPU time to the session median GPU time is calculated first, followed by the median of those five ratios. The results are 0.5404, 0.7137, 0.8240, and 0.9125, respectively. The CPU transform is faster in all five sessions at each batch size. The source is the locally archived \texttt{t4-phase2-study} campaign, with seeds 2003--2007. Its ratios do not replace the GTX CPU baseline and are not pooled with the GTX results into a universal speedup factor.

\subsection{Conditional Compute-Time Cost Model}
Let $h_C$ and $h_G$ denote the consistently attributable hourly cost of the CPU and GPU paths, respectively (including the required host resources for the GPU). For repeated processing at the measured throughput $R_j$, the modeled duration per decimal GB and variable cost are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cost}t_j=\frac{1000}{R_j},\qquad c_j=\frac{h_jt_j}{3600}.\end{equation}
This model assumes that processing services are charged in proportion to occupied time and that the hourly costs of both paths are consistently attributed to the same task and billing period. For the GPU path, attributable cost includes the required host; for the CPU path, the capacity used must be clearly defined. The model excludes minimum billing intervals, queueing, and utilization adjustments, and does not estimate actual prices, electricity consumption, or CAPEX. The condition $c_G<c_C$ implies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:threshold}\frac{h_G}{h_C}<\frac{R_G}{R_C}.\end{equation}
For a transparent sensitivity analysis, $h_C=1$ normalized unit per hour is assumed and $h_G/h_C\in\{0.50;0.75;1.00;1.25;1.50;2.00\}$ is examined. These are \emph{assumed relative prices}, not euros or dollars. A separate model for a potential fixed investment is $\Delta C(V)=F_G-F_C+(c_G-c_C)V$; without observed fixed costs and processing volumes, an actual break-even point cannot be calculated.

At 32 MiB, according to Equation~\eqref{eq:threshold}, the attributable hourly price of processing through the GPU path must be less than 82.73\% of the corresponding CPU price for its modeled variable cost per GB to be lower. This is a \emph{relative service-price threshold}, not evidence of monetary savings, a discount on the device purchase price, or a percentage reduction in business expenditure. The conclusion holds under proportional time-based charging and consistently defined attributable costs. In business practice, CPU capacity, the GPU device, and the required host can have different fixed and variable cost structures, capacity reservations, and billing intervals. Therefore, the hourly price of one CPU core cannot be compared directly with the price of an entire GPU server without matching the attributed resources. At equal hourly prices defined in this way, the CPU has a lower modeled variable cost. If the GPU hourly cost is only 75\% of the CPU cost, the conditional model may favor the GPU for the three sizes shown, but the borderline 16 MiB case is sensitive to session variability. The medians of the session-paired time thresholds are 0.9529, 0.7496, and 0.8233, differing from the ratios of aggregated throughputs, with a particularly wider range at 16 MiB.\par
A lower modeled cost does not in itself establish the case for a near-term investment: GPU acquisition, depreciation, energy, maintenance, and actual utilization have not been quantified. When both the modeled variable costs and a nonnegative difference in fixed costs favor the CPU, no positive break-even volume for GPU investment exists in this linear scenario.

\subsection{Supplementary T4 Page-Processing Example}
The separate T4 experiment shows that the median of session CPU/GPU time ratios approaches unity as the number of independent pages increases: from 0.5404 for one page to 0.9125 for 32 pages. The GPU pipeline thus narrows its relative gap to the tested CPU EVP transform, but does not outperform it at any of the four batch sizes. For 32 pages, a CPU/GPU time ratio of 0.9125 corresponds to approximately 8.75\% shorter CPU time relative to GPU time under the estimator used. This is not a percentage calculated by pooling all repetitions. At equal attributable hourly costs, the same time-based model yields a lower variable cost for the CPU path within this separate comparison. The finding neither establishes the cause of the convergence nor permits extrapolation of a crossover point beyond the tested range.\par

\section{Discussion, Limitations, and Future Work}
The practical lesson from the experiments is that technical choices should be based on data location, measurement scope, and available alternatives. When data originate in system memory, preparation and copying costs can offset the GPU kernel's advantage. When data are already on the GPU, resident throughput can be useful, but the cost of generating those data, subsequent output, and other processing must be attributed to the same business task. The standard AES-NI path remains a strong baseline, but studying a single sequential path does not measure the CPU's full multicore capacity.\par
There are four limitations. First, the primary results come from one Windows x64 machine with consumer hardware, while the T4 results come from a different, heterogeneous campaign. Second, the GPU code serves as a validated experimental baseline, not evidence of a production-optimal CUDA implementation. Third, storage I/O, the full application protocol, and authentication are excluded; CTR alone does not provide integrity, so the results cannot be transferred directly to a complete AES-GCM service \citep{nist_gcm2007}. Fourth, neither energy-consumption measurements nor documented market prices are available; the economic results are normalized and conditional.\par
For one-off processing of buffers in system memory, the observed GTX campaign favors the existing single-stream CPU AES-NI path in execution time and, at equal attributable hourly cost, in modeled variable cost. This does not imply that GPU procurement cannot be justified: a procurement decision requires separate data on fixed costs, other uses of the device, and utilization. When encryption is a stage within a broader GPU workflow, resident throughput indicates potential but does not demonstrate a lower price for the complete service.\par
The T4 findings inform the discussion of batch-processing economics only within their own timing boundaries. Differences in CPU host, operating system, library, and output-allocation policy prevent the ratio's approach to unity from being interpreted as a common GTX/T4 speedup curve. Synthetic independent pages are not a Parquet implementation: the file format, compression, authentication of other modules, storage workflow, and key management are not included. These results therefore do not establish the economic viability of GPUs for Parquet.\par
For the economics of business information systems, future work should first examine the application-level workflow for encrypting independent pages (e.g., Parquet), total cost of ownership, and accounting for host/GPU resources over a common period. A second direction is to measure energy and resource occupancy before considering equipment-procurement or cloud-rental scenarios. Streaming, overlapping copying with execution, and a multiworker CPU baseline are technically relevant, but these experiments are not part of this paper. Economic viability is therefore not generalized to GPU servers or the wider market without new data.\par


\section{Conclusion}
The results show that, for one-off AES-128-CTR processing of data from system memory on the tested Windows x64/i5-10300H/GTX 1650 Ti platform, single-stream CPU AES-NI is a faster execution path than the tested CUDA pipeline at 1, 16, and 32 MiB. At equal attributable hourly cost, the CPU also achieves a lower modeled variable cost per GB. At the same time, the resident GPU kernel at 32 MiB achieves 4800.6 MB/s compared with the CPU's 1455.8 MB/s, or about 3.30 times the throughput, but without data transfers. This indicates processing potential when data are already on the device, rather than an advantage for the complete business workflow.\par
The separate Linux/T4 campaign with CPU OpenSSL EVP shows that increasing the number of independent pages from 1 to 32 reduces the GPU pipeline's relative gap under the observed estimator. Nevertheless, all four tested batch sizes remain slower than the CPU EVP transform in all five sessions. For 32 pages, the median of session CPU/GPU time ratios is 0.9125, meaning that CPU time is approximately 8.75\% shorter relative to GPU time under that estimator. This campaign remains separate from the GTX measurements and is not evidence of the cost-effectiveness of GPU processing of Parquet files.\par
The paper's economic contribution is not proof of the universal cost-effectiveness of either architecture, but the identification of conditions under which the choice of execution path can reduce modeled processing cost. The relative GPU/CPU hourly-price thresholds derived from aggregated GTX pipeline throughputs are 0.9503, 0.7626, and 0.8273 for 1, 16, and 32 MiB, respectively. They hold under the defined timing boundaries and consistent attribution of resources, including the required GPU host. Without documented prices, fixed costs, energy use, and utilization, they do not represent actual bills, realized savings, or a conclusion about the justification for GPU procurement. The experiments and derived model support a conditional execution-path decision; broader application-level and investment conclusions remain outside the scope of this paper.

\section*{SUMMARY}
This study presents validated AES-128-CTR encryption experiments on a Windows x64 machine with consumer hardware and derives a conditional resource-time cost model. The main experiment compares a single-stream CPU AES-NI transform with CUDA kernel-only and prepared host-device pipeline paths. At 32 MiB the GPU kernel reached 4800.6 MB/s, but its transfer-inclusive pipeline reached 1204.5 MB/s, below the CPU's 1455.8 MB/s. A separate Tesla T4 study using independent 1 MiB pages and OpenSSL EVP is reported only as contextual evidence because it has different software, host hardware and timing boundaries.\par
To connect these technical results with infrastructure decisions, the paper derives normalized resource-time cost per decimal GB and the corresponding threshold for the GPU-to-CPU attributable hourly-cost ratio. For 1, 16 and 32 MiB the rate-based GPU pipeline thresholds are 0.9503, 0.7626 and 0.8273. At equal normalized hourly charges, the CPU path has lower modeled variable cost in these observations. The 0.8273 value at 32 MiB is a relative service-price threshold, not evidence of monetary savings. It assumes charges proportional to occupied time and consistent attribution of CPU capacity and the GPU together with its required host. In practice, these resources can have different fixed and variable costs, capacity reservations, and billing intervals; unmatched CPU-core and whole GPU-server prices cannot be compared directly. The model does not establish actual business expenditure or procurement savings.\par
For the tested host-memory workload, the CPU AES-NI path is faster at 1, 16, and 32 MiB and has lower modeled variable cost when attributable hourly charges are equal. Resident GPU processing offers compute-only potential, with about 3.30 times the throughput at 32 MiB, rather than an observed application-level saving. In the separate Linux/T4 campaign, the median of five session CPU/GPU time ratios increases from 0.5404 to 0.9125 as the batch grows from one to 32 pages. The CPU EVP transform remains faster in all five sessions at each tested batch size. At 32 pages, the ratio corresponds to approximately 8.75\% shorter CPU time relative to GPU time under that estimator. Different CPU implementations, output-allocation policies, operating systems, and hosts preclude pooling these campaigns.\par
A practical motivation is Apache Parquet modular encryption, which may use CTR for data pages and GCM for metadata. Synthetic page measurements do not benchmark Parquet itself or establish its GPU cost-effectiveness. The contribution is the identification of conditions under which an execution-path choice can reduce modeled processing cost, not a universal verdict on either architecture or GPU procurement. The conducted experiments provide the technical basis for the conditional resource-cost model. Broader claims about application performance, energy, actual savings, or investment returns would require evidence beyond the present study.
