
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented a validation-first, reproducible methodological framework for benchmarking symmetric cryptographic primitives in heterogeneous CPU--GPU computing environments. The primary contribution lies not in the development of a new cryptographic algorithm or optimization technique, but in a structured experimental protocol that integrates functional validation, controlled workloads, explicitly defined measurement boundaries, paired performance comparisons, session-level aggregation, and preservation of experimental artifacts. The framework provides a systematic basis for interpreting the performance of specific cryptographic implementations under documented hardware and software conditions.

The experimental results demonstrate that the choice of measurement scope can substantially alter conclusions regarding heterogeneous cryptographic performance. Hardware-accelerated AES-NI execution achieved approximately 2.4--2.8 times the throughput of the corresponding conventional software AES implementations in the evaluated CBC configurations. In the principal AES-128 CPU--GPU comparison, the CUDA baseline achieved resident throughput of 4800.6 MB/s for a 32 MiB CTR workload, compared with 1455.8 MB/s for the CPU AES-NI reference. However, when host-to-device transfer, kernel execution, device-to-host transfer, and synchronization were included in the measured GPU pipeline, throughput decreased to 1204.5 MB/s. Thus, the isolated computational advantage of the GPU did not translate into an end-to-end advantage under the tested synchronous execution configuration.

The evaluation of a lightweight CUDA optimization candidate further demonstrated that improvements in isolated kernel execution do not necessarily produce corresponding improvements in transfer-inclusive performance. Memory behavior, data movement, synchronization, and runtime overhead may influence the overall result independently of kernel-level throughput. These observations reinforce the importance of reporting resident-kernel and end-to-end pipeline measurements separately, rather than treating them as interchangeable indicators of accelerator performance.

The retrospective DES/TDEA comparisons, reduced-keyspace experiment, and avalanche analysis provided complementary methodological examples while remaining outside the central heterogeneous-performance contribution. In particular, fixed-key encryption throughput must not be interpreted as exhaustive key-search throughput, and avalanche measurements alone do not establish overall cryptographic security. These distinctions illustrate the broader importance of matching experimental metrics to the specific properties or operations being evaluated.

The findings are limited to the evaluated cryptographic library paths, CPU and GPU implementations, hardware platform, workload sizes, and synchronous CUDA processing pipeline. They do not establish a general upper bound on GPU AES throughput or demonstrate that CPU AES-NI is universally faster than GPU-based encryption. Moreover, the current study does not isolate the individual contributions of data-transfer, synchronization, and operating-system overhead, and its five-session statistical design limits broader generalization.

Future work will prioritize component-level pipeline measurements, asynchronous data transfers using \texttt{cudaMemcpyAsync}, pinned-memory buffering, and multiple CUDA streams. Additional experiments will investigate a wider range of workload sizes, independent native CPU AES-NI references, and a second experimental platform based on an NVIDIA T4 GPU under Linux. These measurements will support the development and validation of a quantitative execution-cost model and the identification of possible CPU--GPU crossover points. Further extensions may examine optimized CUDA AES kernels, GPU AES-GCM, and architecture-specific profiling using NVIDIA performance-analysis tools.

Overall, the study demonstrates that meaningful cryptographic performance evaluation requires more than comparing isolated throughput values. Functional correctness, execution boundaries, hardware acceleration, data residency, statistical treatment, and reproducible experimental records must be considered together. The central result is therefore methodological: the apparent performance advantage of a cryptographic implementation depends not only on its computational capabilities, but also on what is measured and how the complete execution path is defined.
