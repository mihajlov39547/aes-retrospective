\section{Related Work and Research Gap}
\label{sec:related_work}

Research on the acceleration of symmetric cryptography has progressed along two complementary
directions. The first investigates algorithmic and implementation techniques for improving AES
throughput on parallel hardware. The second addresses the methodological requirements for
comparing heterogeneous computing systems, including the choice of CPU reference, data-transfer
overhead, timing boundaries, experimental variability, and reproducibility.

These directions are closely related but do not answer the same research question. A highly
optimized GPU kernel may demonstrate the computational potential of a particular architecture
without establishing that transferring data to and from the device is advantageous for an
application. Conversely, a transfer-inclusive comparison may reveal important system-level costs
without identifying the maximum achievable performance of the underlying GPU.

The present study builds on both research directions. It evaluates validated AES-128
implementations across three heterogeneous configurations, explicitly separating software CPU
execution, hardware-assisted AES-NI, device-resident CUDA processing, and a synchronous
prepared-buffer GPU pipeline.

The purpose of this section is to position the study relative to previous AES acceleration
research, identify the relevant methodological limitations of performance comparisons, and
define the specific research gap addressed by the experimental evaluation.

\subsection{GPU Acceleration of AES}
\label{subsec:gpu_aes_literature}

The acceleration of the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) on graphics processing units has been
investigated for nearly two decades.

Early studies established that programmable GPUs could exploit the data-level parallelism
available in symmetric block-cipher operations \cite{manavski2007cuda,harrison2007aes}.

The suitability of a cryptographic workload for parallel execution depends partly on its mode of
operation.

Electronic Codebook (ECB) mode permits independent encryption of individual plaintext blocks.
Counter (CTR) mode similarly permits independent generation of keystream blocks from distinct
counter values.

By contrast, Cipher Block Chaining (CBC) encryption introduces a dependency between consecutive
ciphertext blocks, limiting direct parallelization within a single message. CBC decryption
permits parallel block-cipher evaluation when the corresponding ciphertext blocks are available
\cite{dworkin2001modes}.

These differences explain why ECB and CTR frequently appear in GPU AES performance studies. ECB
remains unsuitable for the protection of general structured application data, however, and its
use as a performance benchmark should not be interpreted as a security recommendation.

Manavski~\cite{manavski2007cuda} demonstrated an early CUDA-oriented AES implementation,
contributing to the investigation of programmable GPUs as cryptographic accelerators.

Harrison and Waldron~\cite{harrison2007aes} also investigated AES encryption and decryption on
GPU architectures, providing early evidence that general-purpose graphics hardware could support
symmetric cryptography.

Iwai et al.~\cite{iwai2012acceleration} subsequently studied the effects of thread granularity
and memory placement on AES execution using an NVIDIA GeForce GTX~285.

Their experiments identified a processing granularity of 16 bytes per thread as favorable for
the evaluated implementation, achieving approximately 35~Gbit/s.

Importantly, they also investigated overlapping computation and data transfers, reporting
approximately 22.5~Gbit/s when transfer time was included.

This work demonstrates that the importance of host--device communication was recognized in early
CUDA AES research.

The separation of device computation and transfer-inclusive performance is therefore not
introduced here as a previously unknown concept.

Later studies investigated increasingly specialized implementation strategies.

Wang and Chu~\cite{wang2019gpu} examined GPU AES encryption and decryption using
lookup-table-based transformations and independent AES states assigned to GPU execution threads.

Their work illustrates how representation of the cipher state, memory access, and thread-level
organization influence the performance of CUDA implementations.

Tezcan~\cite{tezcan2021optimization} investigated shared-memory optimizations for GPU AES,
including techniques designed to eliminate memory-bank conflicts.

The reported AES-128 encryption throughput reached 878.6~Gbit/s on an NVIDIA RTX~2070~Super.

This result illustrates the substantial potential of architecture-aware GPU optimization, but it
should not be directly compared with transfer-inclusive throughput measured on different
implementations and hardware.

More recent bit-sliced approaches have further investigated the relationship between AES
representation and GPU parallelism.

A 2024 study~\cite{bitsliced2024gpu} reported high-throughput AES-ECB and AES-CTR execution
using bit-sliced implementations, including results on an RTX~3080 and an embedded Jetson Orin
Nano GPU.

The study demonstrates the continuing relevance of representation-specific optimizations and
parallel counter generation in GPU AES research.

Taken together, these studies establish that GPU AES throughput can be improved substantially
through memory organization, parallel granularity, instruction use, and algorithm
representation.

Nevertheless, reported performance figures must be interpreted within their respective
experimental conditions.

Results obtained using different GPU architectures, AES modes, workload sizes, software
implementations, and timing boundaries cannot be treated as directly interchangeable
measurements.

The present work does not seek to reproduce state-of-the-art optimized GPU AES implementations
or claim record-setting encryption throughput.

Instead, a validated CUDA baseline and a lightweight optimized-v1 candidate are used to
investigate how the observed CPU--GPU relationship changes with workload size, cipher mode,
computing environment, and measured execution scope.

\subsection{CPU Acceleration and Fair Heterogeneous Comparisons}
\label{subsec:cpu_gpu_comparison}

A fundamental issue in assessing GPU acceleration is the choice of CPU reference implementation.

Modern x86 processors commonly provide dedicated Advanced Encryption Standard New Instructions
(AES-NI), which accelerate important AES transformations through processor-supported
operations~\cite{intel2012aesni}.

The performance of an AES-NI-enabled implementation can differ substantially from that of a
conventional software implementation.

Consequently, an apparent GPU advantage over software AES does not necessarily establish an
advantage over a modern hardware-accelerated CPU.

A meaningful CPU--GPU comparison should therefore identify the cryptographic library, hardware
acceleration path, execution scope, and relevant threading assumptions.

This concern is not specific to cryptography.

Lee et al.~\cite{lee2010debunking} investigated CPU--GPU performance comparisons and
demonstrated that large reported GPU speedups can decrease substantially when the CPU
implementation is appropriately optimized.

Their findings establish a broader methodological principle: comparisons between heterogeneous
architectures are sensitive to the quality of the implementations selected as references.

The present study applies this principle by reporting two distinct CPU execution paths.

The conventional software AES implementation provides a reference for assessing the impact of
software-level cryptographic computation.

The AES-NI-enabled implementation provides a stronger hardware-assisted CPU reference for
evaluating GPU offloading.

The AES-NI path is used as the principal CPU denominator in the paired GPU speedup analysis.

However, the CPU reference is not presented as the maximum attainable AES performance of the
entire host system.

Library choice, mode-specific processing, processor features, instruction dispatch, thread
count, and invocation overhead can affect the result.

In particular, a single-threaded library execution path should not be interpreted as equivalent
to a fully optimized multithreaded CPU encryption service.

Accordingly, the present results characterize measured implementation paths rather than
universal architectural performance limits.

\subsection{Measurement Scope and Data-Movement Overhead}
\label{subsec:measurement_scope}

Another central methodological issue concerns the operations included within a timed interval.

A GPU application may involve input preparation, host-buffer staging, host-to-device transfer,
kernel dispatch, device execution, synchronization, device-to-host transfer, and subsequent
application processing.

Whether these operations are included in a benchmark determines the meaning of the reported
elapsed time.

Gregg and Hazelwood \cite{gregg2011data} demonstrated the importance of data location in
meaningful CPU--GPU comparisons.

Their work emphasizes that application-level performance cannot be inferred solely from device
computation when substantial data movement is required.

CUDA programming guidance similarly identifies minimizing unnecessary transfers and organizing
computation and data movement as important optimization considerations
\cite{nvidiaProgrammingGuide, nvidiaBestPractices,harris2012overlap}.

CuPy documentation further emphasizes that CUDA operations may execute asynchronously relative
to the host, making appropriate device timing and synchronization essential
\cite{cupyPerformance,cupyRawKernel}.

These considerations are particularly important for cryptographic workloads because the input
and output buffers may be maintained in CPU memory rather than resident GPU memory.

A resident kernel measurement characterizes device execution after the necessary input data have
been placed on the GPU.

A transfer-inclusive pipeline measurement additionally captures the defined host-driven
operations needed to move data through the accelerator.

The two measurements therefore answer different computational questions.

For a sequential GPU pipeline, the elapsed time may be represented conceptually as

\begin{equation}
  T_{\mathrm{pipeline}}
  =
  T_{\mathrm{H2D}}
  +
  T_{\mathrm{kernel}}
  +
  T_{\mathrm{D2H}}
  +
  T_{\mathrm{overhead}},
  \label{eq:gpu_e2e_components}
\end{equation}

where the terms denote non-overlapping conceptual contributions from transfer, kernel execution,
and other operations within the chosen measurement boundary.

This expression is a conceptual decomposition, not a claim that separately measured component
medians must sum to the observed median pipeline time.

The overhead term may include dispatch, synchronization, and relevant host-side operations,
depending on the implementation.

Operations outside the timer must also be identified.

In the present study, the measured GPU pipeline starts with prepared pinned host buffers and
preallocated device buffers.

The interval includes the defined synchronous H2D--kernel--D2H sequence and the synchronization
required for completion.

It does not include initial host staging, buffer allocation or pinning, cryptographic key
preparation, or complete application I/O.

Therefore, the term \emph{prepared-buffer pipeline} is preferred over an unqualified claim of
complete application-level end-to-end performance.

This explicit boundary makes the comparison reproducible and helps prevent resident-kernel
throughput from being misinterpreted as the throughput of a complete encryption application.

\subsection{Scientific Benchmarking and Reproducibility}
\label{subsec:benchmarking_literature}

The reliability of performance comparisons depends on experimental design as well as
implementation efficiency.

Mytkowicz et al. \cite{mytkowicz2009wrong} demonstrated that seemingly minor experimental
conditions can introduce systematic bias and substantially alter benchmark conclusions.

Their work highlights the importance of documenting execution conditions and potential sources
of measurement interference.

Kalibera and Jones \cite{kalibera2013rigorous} examined rigorous benchmarking under practical
time constraints and emphasized the need to account for variability across different levels of
experimental replication.

Hoefler and Belli \cite{hoefler2015scientific} further discussed scientific benchmarking of
parallel computing systems, stressing defensible performance reporting and the interpretation of
measurement variability.

These studies motivate a hierarchical treatment of benchmark observations rather than
indiscriminate pooling of all repeated timing values.

The present study organizes observations into independent study sessions.

Each selected platform contributes five sessions, and each measured group contains twenty
recorded timing repetitions following five warm-up executions.

The session constitutes the primary replication unit for statistical aggregation.

CPU--GPU speedups are calculated from paired session-level timing medians and subsequently
aggregated across the five sessions.

This preserves the workload pairing and avoids treating nested repetitions as independent
experimental sessions.

The framework additionally retains raw observations, derived summaries, validation records,
source snapshots, and execution metadata.

An independent offline analysis reconstructed the saved statistical estimators from the archived
raw data without detecting numerical discrepancies within the declared comparison tolerances.

This verification supports the internal consistency and auditability of the analysis.

However, successful cryptographic validation does not independently establish the absence of
timing bias.

Likewise, matching source hashes do not imply identical compiled binaries or equivalent
execution conditions across different platforms.

The methodology therefore distinguishes cryptographic correctness, measurement integrity,
repeatability, and the external validity of the resulting performance conclusions.

\subsection{Research Gap and Research Questions}
\label{subsec:research_gap}

The reviewed literature establishes two relevant bodies of knowledge.

The first demonstrates the potential of GPU AES acceleration through parallel execution, memory
organization, table-based transformations, bit slicing, and architecture-specific optimizations
\cite{iwai2012acceleration, wang2019gpu, tezcan2021optimization, bitsliced2024gpu}.

The second establishes methodological requirements for heterogeneous performance comparisons,
including appropriate CPU references, data-movement costs, experimental variability, and
reproducible benchmarking practices
\cite{lee2010debunking, gregg2011data, mytkowicz2009wrong, kalibera2013rigorous, hoefler2015scientific}.

These principles are already well established.

In particular, previous AES-specific research has considered transfer overhead and overlap
between computation and communication \cite{iwai2012acceleration}.

Therefore, the present work does not claim to have discovered data-transfer overhead or
introduced the kernel-only versus pipeline distinction.

Instead, the specific research gap addressed here is the need for an integrated,
validation-first, auditable empirical comparison that simultaneously includes:

\begin{enumerate}

  \item A conventional software AES reference and a hardware-assisted CPU AES-NI reference;

  \item Validated baseline and optimized-v1 CUDA AES-128 implementations;

  \item Matched ECB and CTR workloads across multiple input sizes;

  \item Explicitly distinguished resident and transfer-inclusive GPU measurement scopes;

  \item Independent study sessions, paired statistical estimators, and reported variability;

  \item Preserved raw data, configuration metadata, validation artifacts, and source-level
    provenance;

  \item Observations from two accessible NVIDIA laptop GPUs and a Linux-based datacenter GPU
    configuration.

\end{enumerate}

The objective is not to demonstrate a universal architectural advantage, but to identify
conditions under which particular CPU--GPU performance conclusions are supported by reproducible
evidence.

The overarching research question is formulated as follows:

\begin{quote}
  \textbf{Central Research Question:}

  Under which measured conditions can GPU-based AES-128 encryption outperform a
  hardware-accelerated CPU AES-NI implementation, and how does the definition of the measurement
  boundary affect that conclusion?
\end{quote}

This question is operationalized through three complementary research questions:

\begin{enumerate}

  \item[\textbf{RQ1:}] \textbf{GPU offloading advantage.} Under which tested input sizes, AES
    modes, and hardware configurations do CUDA implementations outperform software CPU AES and
    hardware-assisted AES-NI, and which advantages remain consistent across the five study
    sessions?

  \item[\textbf{RQ2:}] \textbf{Measurement-scope dependence.} How does the observed CPU--GPU
    performance relationship change when the GPU boundary is expanded from device-resident
    kernel execution to the synchronous prepared-buffer pipeline including host--device
    transfers and synchronization?

  \item[\textbf{RQ3:}] \textbf{Consistency and cross-platform interpretation.} How do session
    variability, workload size, cipher mode, and differences among the tested Windows laptop and
    Linux/T4 configurations limit the generalization of observed GPU acceleration?

\end{enumerate}

These questions are intended to be answered empirically using the validated and archived study
results.

They do not require an assumption that GPU acceleration must be advantageous.

Both positive and negative outcomes are relevant to the research problem.

\subsection{Contributions and Positioning}
\label{subsec:related_contributions}

Relative to the reviewed literature, the study makes three principal contributions.

\begin{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{An integrated, validation-first, reproducible benchmarking methodology.}

  The framework combines cryptographic correctness verification, deterministic paired inputs,
  explicit timing scopes, session-level statistical aggregation, and preservation of raw
  experimental evidence.

  Its methodological contribution lies in applying these requirements together, not in claiming
  that the individual benchmarking principles are unprecedented.

  \item \textbf{A three-platform empirical assessment of contemporary AES execution paths.}

  The study evaluates software AES, AES-NI, and two CUDA candidates across fifteen sessions and
  18,000 raw timing observations.

  The platforms include a consumer GeForce GTX~1650~Ti, an entry-level professional T600 Laptop
  GPU, and a Tesla T4 datacenter GPU.

  The analysis preserves platform-specific CPU references and does not pool heterogeneous
  systems into a single performance estimate.

  \item \textbf{An evidence-qualified interpretation of GPU acceleration and deployment
    relevance.}

  The results identify conditions under which GPU execution exceeds the selected AES-NI CPU
  reference, including a consistent transfer-inclusive CTR advantage on the T600 at 32~MiB.

  They also document resident-to-pipeline reversals, negative or mixed CPU--GPU comparisons, and
  mode-dependent performance differences.

  These observations support practical interpretation without claiming universal GPU superiority
  or complete application-level performance.

\end{enumerate}

The study therefore occupies a distinct position between GPU kernel optimization research and
general performance-measurement methodology.

Its scientific value lies in connecting validated cryptographic execution, clearly defined
timing boundaries, a strong CPU hardware baseline, and independently auditable experimental
results.

This approach is particularly relevant to heterogeneous data-processing and communication
systems, where the appropriate cryptographic execution path may depend on data residency,
workload size, required functionality, and the acceleration mechanisms available on the host.

The resulting methodology provides a basis for informed architectural assessment rather than a
predetermined recommendation to favor either CPU or GPU execution.
