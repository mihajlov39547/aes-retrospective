More than two decades after the standardization of the Advanced
Encryption Standard (AES), the growing availability of GPU
acceleration raises a practical question: under which conditions
can GPUs outperform modern CPUs equipped with dedicated AES
instructions? Motivated by the historical transition from DES
and Triple DES to AES, this paper presents a validation-first,
reproducible benchmarking framework for evaluating AES-128
encryption across heterogeneous CPU and NVIDIA GPU environments.
The study compares software CPU implementations, hardware-
accelerated AES-NI, and two CUDA implementations in ECB and
CTR modes, explicitly distinguishing device-resident kernel
execution from synchronous GPU processing that includes
host--device transfers and synchronization.

Experiments were conducted on two Windows laptop configurations
equipped with 4 GB NVIDIA GPUs (GeForce GTX 1650 Ti and T600
Laptop GPU) and a Linux-based NVIDIA Tesla T4 datacenter
configuration. The evaluation comprises 15 independent study
sessions and 18,000 timing observations, with paired workloads,
cryptographic correctness validation, and independently verified
statistical aggregation.

At 32 MiB, GPU resident execution outperformed the corresponding
AES-NI CPU implementation in all five sessions on every platform.
More importantly, the transfer-inclusive pipeline achieved a
1.35-fold speedup over AES-NI on the T600 Laptop GPU and a
4.76-fold speedup on the Tesla T4 for AES-128 CTR, with the
advantage reproduced in all five sessions. However, the consumer
GTX 1650 Ti showed no consistently positive pipeline advantage
over AES-NI, while the T4 ECB pipeline reached only 0.89 times
the corresponding CPU performance. These findings demonstrate
that GPU acceleration can outperform hardware-assisted CPU
encryption even when data transfers are included, but that the
benefit depends on workload size, cipher mode, host CPU, and
measurement boundaries.

The study provides reproducible evidence for evaluating GPU
offloading on accessible and datacenter hardware and establishes
a practical basis for selecting cryptographic execution paths
in heterogeneous data-processing and communication systems.
