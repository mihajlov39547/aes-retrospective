\section{Experimental Results and Interpretation}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Implementation-path sensitivity}
Table~\ref{tab:impl} summarizes AES-128-CTR throughput at 32 MiB. On Windows, the OpenSSL transform rate is 5537 MB/s, compared with 2877 MB/s for PyCryptodome. On Arm64, default OpenSSL reaches 4030 MB/s, whereas the masked OpenSSL configuration and PyCryptodome achieve 218 and 209 MB/s, respectively. These values are medians across five sessions for each configuration.

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\caption{AES-128-CTR, 32 MiB: throughput by platform, implementation and timing scope (decimal MB/s).}\label{tab:impl}\small
\begin{tabular}{llrr}\toprule
Host & Implementation & Transform & Full call\\\midrule
Windows i5-10300H & OpenSSL EVP & 5537 & 1479\\
Windows i5-10300H & PyCryptodome & 2877 & 1202\\
Oracle Arm64 & OpenSSL default & 4030 & ---\\
Oracle Arm64 & OpenSSL armcap=0 & 218 & ---\\
Oracle Arm64 & PyCryptodome & 209 & ---\\\bottomrule\end{tabular}
\par\smallskip\parbox{\linewidth}{\footnotesize Transform and full-call rates use the intervals defined in Section~\ref{sec:method}. Full call denotes the instrumented operation. Dashes separate the Arm64 transform comparison from the operation-level worker analysis below.}
\end{table}

On Arm64, the default OpenSSL/PyCryptodome throughput ratio is approximately $19.3\times$, while the default/masked OpenSSL ratio is $18.5\times$. On Windows, the transform-scope OpenSSL/PyCryptodome contrast is approximately $1.93\times$. These are implementation/interface throughput contrasts, not isolated AES instruction speedups. In particular, \texttt{OPENSSL\_armcap=0} changes multiple hardware-optimized execution capabilities rather than selectively disabling AES instructions.

The Arm64 difference is consistent across the five recorded sessions. Default OpenSSL session medians range from 4018 to 4035 MB/s, compared with 217.4--218.4 MB/s for masked OpenSSL and 208.8--209.5 MB/s for PyCryptodome. Thus, the approximately nineteen-fold separation is substantially larger than the observed session variation. It demonstrates sensitivity to implementation and capability configuration on this cloud instance, but does not isolate one instruction or establish an architectural advantage of Arm64 over x86-64.

Figure~\ref{fig:size} places the 32 MiB results in the context of message size. Larger payloads sustain substantially higher OpenSSL throughput than the alternative paths on each host. At 1 KiB, the native transform interval is very short, making the calculated rate more sensitive to timer resolution and call overhead. Large-buffer throughput should consequently not be used to predict the latency of short requests. The two panels describe separate software environments; differences between them are not estimates of an intrinsic x86-64 versus Arm64 performance ratio.

\subsection{Attenuation of implementation advantages}
The Windows measurements provide the central comparison across timing boundaries. At 32 MiB, OpenSSL achieves 5537 MB/s for the transform and 1479 MB/s for the instrumented operation; PyCryptodome achieves 2877 and 1202 MB/s, respectively. The OpenSSL/PyCryptodome ratio decreases from approximately $1.93\times$ to $1.23\times$ (unrounded ratios 1.9247 and 1.2299). The broader OpenSSL session medians range from 1452 to 1625 MB/s, while the PyCryptodome medians range from 1179 to 1263 MB/s.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/windows_boundaries.pdf}
\caption{Attenuation of the OpenSSL advantage for Windows AES-128-CTR at 32 MiB. (a) Throughput at each measured boundary. (b) OpenSSL/PyCryptodome ratios calculated from unrounded campaign aggregates. Retention is operation throughput divided by transform throughput, not a component-level overhead measurement.}\label{fig:windows}\end{figure}

Expressed as a proportion of transform throughput, the broader operation retains 26.7\% for OpenSSL and 41.8\% for PyCryptodome. Figure~\ref{fig:windows} distinguishes this within-library retention from the between-library throughput ratio. The former describes how much throughput remains when the timed scope expands; the latter shows how much relative advantage remains over the alternative implementation. OpenSSL remains faster in this benchmark, but its approximately 93\% transform-level advantage becomes approximately 23\% at the broader boundary.

These observations quantify attenuation without identifying its component causes. The native OpenSSL transform excludes the outer Python entry, whereas the PyCryptodome transform includes Python dispatch and its internal foreign-function interface. The broader operation adds preparation, allocation, bindings, setup, result conversion and diagnostics, with implementation-specific costs. The retention ratios cannot establish how much each component contributes, and subtracting aggregate medians would not provide a valid decomposition. In particular, they do not show that allocation alone accounts for the difference or that the library ranking would reverse at another interface.

The practical result is that an implementation advantage measured for prepared encryption processing need not transfer proportionally to a surrounding software operation. The instrumented operation is broader than the transform, but it remains a benchmark call rather than production end-to-end latency. Context reuse, buffer reuse and alternative bindings are consequently candidates for controlled follow-up experiments, not improvements already demonstrated by these measurements.

As a secondary operation-level observation, the verified Oracle archives yield 1059.6 MB/s for one worker and 2031.1 MB/s in aggregate for two workers, a descriptive ratio of approximately $1.92\times$. Each worker processes 32 MiB, so the two-worker measurement covers 64 MiB across the common completion interval. This is increased aggregate throughput, not a reduction in the latency of one 32 MiB operation. The campaigns comprise separate sessions and are not statistically paired; the ratio is therefore not a controlled causal estimate of multicore scaling efficiency. Both rates use the instrumented-operation boundary defined in Section~\ref{sec:method}.

\subsection{Key length and authenticated processing}
As supporting workload evidence, the separate CBC experiment shows how key length affects both absolute throughput and relative acceleration (Table~\ref{tab:cbc}). Software encryption decreases from 225.1 MB/s for AES-128 to 174.9 MB/s for AES-256; the AES-NI path decreases from 531.6 to 474.0 MB/s. Nevertheless, the session-based acceleration factor increases from 2.37 to 2.71. Thus, a larger relative benefit from acceleration can coexist with lower absolute throughput. The pattern is consistent with the additional rounds required by longer keys, although the measured factors remain specific to this implementation and mode.

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\caption{AES-CBC encryption at 10 MiB on Windows (MB/s). Speedups are medians of session-level time ratios.}\label{tab:cbc}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}\toprule Key length & Software & AES-NI path & Speedup\\\midrule
AES-128 & 225.1 & 531.6 & 2.37$\times$\\
AES-192 & 198.5 & 504.3 & 2.53$\times$\\
AES-256 & 174.9 & 474.0 & 2.71$\times$\\\bottomrule\end{tabular}\end{table}

Authenticated processing introduces a different workload. Table~\ref{tab:gcm} reports representative GCM sizes, and Fig.~\ref{fig:gcm} includes all five evaluated sizes. At 1 KiB, AES-128 encryption reaches 70.6 MB/s, while verified decryption reaches 38.1 MB/s. At 1 MiB, the corresponding rates rise to 1148.2 and 1131.4 MB/s. At 100 MiB, the corresponding rates are 1179.7 and 1189.3 MB/s. Encryption generates a tag, whereas authenticated decryption verifies it; their API paths therefore have distinct processing requirements, particularly visible for short payloads.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=.88\linewidth]{figures/gcm_payload_size.pdf}
\caption{AES-GCM encryption and verified-decryption throughput on Windows for five payload sizes. Lines connect the measured aggregate rates.}\label{fig:gcm}\end{figure}

This pattern is consistent with fixed processing costs being distributed over more payload bytes as message size increases. The plateau at larger sizes suggests that additional payload then increases processing time approximately in proportion to its length over the measured range. AES-256 generally achieves lower throughput than AES-128, as expected from its additional rounds. However, the experiment does not separately time authentication multiplication, verification or allocation, so it cannot assign the observed size dependence to one component.

These GCM results complement the CTR and CBC observations by showing why a benchmark must represent the required cryptographic service. GCM includes authentication, whereas the CTR and CBC workloads reported here measure confidentiality operations. Their throughput figures therefore describe different functionality. These observations originate from separate campaigns with their own timing conventions; they do not measure an incremental GCM-versus-CTR authentication penalty or isolate authentication overhead. The GCM timer also excludes object construction, so a service handling independent messages must additionally account for setup and its surrounding request-processing costs.
