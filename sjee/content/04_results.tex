
\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section presents the experimental results obtained using the validation-first benchmarking methodology described in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}. The analysis begins with retrospective CPU comparisons of symmetric encryption algorithms, followed by authenticated encryption measurements and the principal paired CPU--GPU evaluation.

The main objective is not to identify a universally superior cryptographic implementation, but to examine how algorithm selection, hardware acceleration, and measurement scope influence the interpretation of observed performance.

Unless otherwise stated, throughput values are expressed in decimal megabytes per second (MB/s), and the reported aggregate values represent the corresponding statistics obtained from five study sessions.

\subsection{CPU Comparison of DES, TDEA, and AES}
\label{subsec:cpu_results}

The initial experiments establish a historical and implementation-oriented reference for symmetric encryption performance. DES, three-key TDEA, and AES-128/192/256 were evaluated in Cipher Block Chaining (CBC) mode without padding.

DES and TDEA are included exclusively for retrospective comparison and are not presented as recommended algorithms for protecting new data. Their historical standardization and subsequent withdrawal or restriction provide context for the evolution of symmetric cryptographic implementations \cite{nist1999des,nbs1980des,barker2017tdea,barker2019transitioning}.

Functional validation employed known-answer CBC test vectors derived from standardized sources \cite{nbs1980des,dworkin2001modes}. The AES experiments additionally distinguished conventional software execution from the hardware-accelerated AES-NI library path.

The results demonstrate substantial implementation-dependent differences in encryption throughput. Among the conventional software paths, AES variants outperform TDEA under the evaluated CBC workload. AES-128 achieves the highest throughput among the software AES configurations, followed by AES-192 and AES-256.

This ordering is consistent with the increasing number of AES rounds associated with longer keys. AES-128, AES-192, and AES-256 require 10, 12, and 14 rounds, respectively \cite{nist2023aes}. However, the measured throughput differences also depend on the library implementation and should not be attributed exclusively to round counts.

Hardware acceleration considerably changes the observed performance relationship. The AES-NI path achieves approximately 2.4--2.8 times the throughput of the corresponding software execution path in the evaluated configurations.

For example, AES-128 CBC encryption increases from 225.1 MB/s to 531.6 MB/s, while AES-256 CBC encryption increases from 174.9 MB/s to 474.0 MB/s.

The relative acceleration is somewhat larger for AES-256 than for AES-128 in the reported measurements. Nevertheless, these factors characterize the specific processor, cryptographic library, workload size, and measurement boundary used in the experiment. They should not be interpreted as universal AES-NI speedup factors.

Importantly, the observed performance differences do not represent comparative cryptographic security. They quantify the execution costs of particular algorithm and implementation configurations.

These findings also motivate the selection of AES-NI, rather than conventional software AES alone, as the primary CPU reference in the subsequent heterogeneous CPU--GPU evaluation.


\subsection{AES-GCM Authenticated Encryption}
\label{subsec:gcm_results}

The second group of experiments extends the CPU analysis to authenticated encryption using AES in Galois/Counter Mode (GCM).

Unlike CBC encryption, which provides confidentiality without integrated authentication, GCM combines encryption with authentication of the ciphertext and optional associated data \cite{dworkin2007gcm}.

The experiment evaluates AES-128-GCM and AES-256-GCM using a 96-bit nonce, a 128-bit authentication tag, and 16 bytes of fixed additional authenticated data (AAD).

Functional validation includes authentication-tag verification and rejection of modified ciphertext, authentication tags, or associated data.

These validation requirements distinguish the GCM workload from confidentiality-only encryption experiments and prevent direct interpretation of CBC and GCM throughput as equivalent cryptographic operations.

Table~\ref{tab:gcm-summary} presents the measured AES-GCM throughput on the selected CPU AES-NI execution path.

\label{tab:gcm-summary}
\begin{tabular}{llrr}
\toprule
Input size & Algorithm & Encryption & Decryption \\
\midrule
1 KiB   & AES-128-GCM & 70.6   & 38.1 \\
1 KiB   & AES-256-GCM & 70.1   & 38.0 \\
1 MiB   & AES-128-GCM & 1148.2 & 1131.4 \\
1 MiB   & AES-256-GCM & 1059.3 & 1059.8 \\

100 MiB & AES-256-GCM & 1100.4 & 1080.4 \\
\bottomrule

\end{table}

The results show a pronounced dependence on input size.

For 1 KiB messages, the reported throughput is substantially lower than for larger inputs. This behavior is consistent with the increased relative contribution of fixed per-operation costs, including invocation overhead and mode-specific processing, when the payload is small.

At 1 MiB and 100 MiB, AES-128-GCM achieves approximately 1.13--1.19 GB/s, while AES-256-GCM achieves approximately 1.06--1.10 GB/s.

The differences between encryption and decryption are relatively small for larger inputs. However, a more pronounced asymmetry is observed for 1 KiB messages, particularly in AES-128-GCM, where encryption reaches 70.6 MB/s and decryption reaches 38.1 MB/s.

The current measurements establish this asymmetry as an observation but do not isolate its cause. Additional profiling would be required to determine the contributions of authentication verification, invocation overhead, and library-specific execution behavior.

For small messages, latency expressed in microseconds per operation would provide a more direct characterization than throughput alone.

These results demonstrate the importance of workload size and cryptographic functionality when interpreting performance measurements. They also establish a contemporary authenticated-encryption reference that complements the subsequent AES-128 ECB/CTR CPU--GPU experiments.

\subsection{CUDA Baseline and Paired CPU--GPU Comparison}
\label{subsec:cpu_gpu_results}

The central experiment evaluates AES-128 execution across conventional CPU software, hardware-accelerated CPU AES-NI, and two CUDA implementation variants.

The CUDA baseline uses a readable byte-oriented AES kernel in which each CUDA thread processes one 16-byte AES block. The execution configuration employs 128 threads per CUDA block.

ECB and CTR modes were selected because they permit independent block-level execution. ECB is used exclusively as a controlled microbenchmark of independent block transformations, while CTR provides a parallelizable counter-based encryption mode defined by NIST SP 800-38A \cite{dworkin2001modes}.

ECB is not presented as a recommended mode for protecting general application data, since independent encryption of identical plaintext blocks can reveal structural information.

The GPU implementations use CUDA execution functionality exposed through CuPy, including custom kernel invocation and CUDA event-based measurement facilities \cite{nvidiaProgrammingGuide,cupyPerformance,cupyRawKernel,okuta2017cupy}.

The paired protocol uses corresponding plaintext data, encryption keys, and initial CTR counter values for each mode, workload size, and repetition.

In addition to the baseline CUDA implementation, a lightweight optimization candidate, designated CUDA v1, is evaluated under the same experimental protocol.

The comparison distinguishes two GPU measurement scopes:

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Resident kernel-only (K):}
The CUDA kernel operates on data already available in device memory. Host--device transfer costs are excluded from the measured kernel execution.

\item \textbf{End-to-end pipeline (E2E):}
The measurement includes the defined host-driven sequence consisting of host-to-device data transfer, CUDA kernel execution, device-to-host transfer, and required synchronization.

\end{enumerate}

The CPU execution paths provide corresponding transform-only references.

The results reveal a consistent distinction between resident GPU throughput and the throughput of the complete measured GPU pipeline.

In all reported configurations, resident CUDA execution achieves higher throughput than the corresponding CPU AES-NI reference.

However, this relationship does not persist when the measured GPU execution includes host--device transfers and synchronization.

For example, in CTR mode with a 1 MiB input, the baseline resident kernel reaches 5017.1 MB/s, compared with 1381.7 MB/s for CPU AES-NI. When the end-to-end GPU pipeline is measured, throughput decreases to 1313.1 MB/s, which is below the AES-NI result.

A similar distinction appears at 32 MiB. The baseline resident CTR throughput reaches 4800.6 MB/s, whereas the corresponding pipeline achieves 1204.5 MB/s. The CPU AES-NI path reaches 1455.8 MB/s for the same workload size.

Thus, the observed GPU computational advantage does not translate into an end-to-end advantage under the tested synchronous pipeline configuration.

This result must be interpreted within the measured execution boundaries. The resident kernel and the CPU transform-only path provide useful comparisons of computational performance, but they do not represent identical application-level processing costs.

The end-to-end GPU measurement includes additional operations required to move data through the accelerator. The comparison therefore illustrates the practical importance of data residency and processing-pipeline organization, consistent with the broader heterogeneous benchmarking literature \cite{gregg2011data}.

Importantly, the observed relationship does not establish an intrinsic limitation of GPU AES encryption. Different transfer configurations, asynchronous execution, stronger CUDA implementations, or alternative platforms could change the measured end-to-end result.

\subsection{Speedup Analysis and Measurement-Scope Dependence}
\label{subsec:speedup_results}

To examine the relative execution costs more directly, GPU speedup factors were calculated using the corresponding CPU AES-NI execution path as the reference.

The speedup statistics were derived from paired session-level timing medians and subsequently aggregated across five study sessions, as defined in Section~\ref{subsec:statistical_analysis}.

A speedup factor greater than one indicates shorter GPU execution time for the corresponding measured scope, whereas a value below one indicates shorter CPU AES-NI execution time.

Table~\ref{tab:gpu-speedups} presents the resulting factors for resident kernel-only (I) and end-to-end pipeline (P) execution.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\small
\caption{GPU speedup relative to CPU AES-NI, calculated as the median of five paired session-level timing ratios. I denotes isolated resident execution and P denotes the end-to-end pipeline. Values greater than one indicate shorter GPU execution time.}
\label{tab:gpu-speedups}
\begin{tabular}{llrrrr}
\toprule
Mode & Size & Baseline I & v1 I & Baseline P & v1 P \\
\midrule
ECB & 1 MiB  & 2.65 & 2.91 & 0.75 & 0.77 \\
ECB & 16 MiB & 1.89 & 1.77 & 0.58 & 0.42 \\
ECB & 32 MiB & 2.50 & 2.52 & 0.63 & 0.64 \\
\midrule
CTR & 1 MiB  & 3.66 & 3.54 & 0.95 & 0.91 \\
CTR & 16 MiB & 2.58 & 2.51 & 0.75 & 0.74 \\
CTR & 32 MiB & 3.30 & 3.40 & 0.82 & 0.83 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The resident measurements indicate substantial acceleration relative to the selected AES-NI CPU reference. Depending on the mode, workload size, and CUDA implementation, the reported resident speedup ranges from approximately 1.77 to 3.66.

However, all reported end-to-end pipeline speedup factors remain below one.

The closest result to parity occurs for the baseline CTR implementation at 1 MiB, where the speedup factor is 0.95. At 32 MiB, the CTR pipeline factors are approximately 0.82--0.83, while the ECB pipeline factors are approximately 0.63--0.64.

These findings demonstrate that the relative ranking of CPU and GPU execution changes when the measurement boundary changes.

A comparison restricted to isolated CUDA kernel execution would suggest a GPU advantage across the evaluated workloads. A transfer-inclusive comparison using the tested synchronous pipeline instead favors the AES-NI CPU reference.

The lightweight CUDA v1 optimization does not provide a consistent performance improvement.

For some resident configurations, the optimized candidate achieves moderately higher throughput than the baseline. In other configurations, the result decreases. The most pronounced difference occurs for ECB at 16 MiB, where the v1 end-to-end pipeline reports lower throughput than the baseline configuration.

Because the optimization is not consistently beneficial and the existing measurements do not isolate the causes of the observed variation, these differences should not be interpreted as evidence of a generally superior CUDA implementation.

The non-monotonic behavior observed around 16 MiB also requires further investigation. Potential contributing factors include execution-environment variability, device clock behavior, memory allocation, transfer characteristics, and synchronization overhead.

Without component-level timing and additional workload-size measurements, assigning the observed reduction to any particular mechanism would be speculative.

The central finding is therefore methodological: the performance relationship between CPU AES-NI and CUDA AES depends on the measured execution scope.

For the tested platform and implementation, high resident GPU throughput does not guarantee higher transfer-inclusive pipeline throughput.

This finding supports the need to report kernel-only and end-to-end measurements separately, particularly when GPU performance is used to motivate architectural decisions in heterogeneous systems.

\subsection{Reduced Keyspace and Avalanche Demonstrations}
\label{subsec:cryptographic_demonstrations}

Two additional experiments investigate conceptual aspects of cryptographic evaluation: exhaustive search over a deliberately reduced keyspace and the avalanche effect.

These experiments serve a demonstrative and retrospective purpose. They are not intended to provide a new cryptanalytic result or to establish the practical security of the evaluated algorithms.


\subsubsection{Reduced-Keyspace Search}

The reduced-keyspace experiment uses AES-128 in ECB mode, with 24 variable key bits and a conventional CPU software execution path.

The timed operation includes candidate-key construction, cipher-object initialization and key scheduling, encryption of two blocks, relevant allocation operations, ciphertext comparison, and termination when a matching candidate is found.

Consequently, the measured candidate-processing rate characterizes the complete implemented search loop rather than the isolated throughput of the AES encryption primitive.

The purpose is to illustrate the growth of exhaustive-search cost with the number of candidate keys, not to estimate the performance of optimized cryptanalytic hardware.

Table~\ref{tab:keyspace} summarizes the experimental scenarios.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\small
\caption{CPU exhaustive-search demonstration over a reduced 24-bit AES-128 keyspace.}
\label{tab:keyspace}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Scenario & Candidates & Time (s) & Candidates/s \\
\midrule
Start  & 1              & 0.0000088 & 113\,636 \\
Middle & 8\,388\,609     & 54.38     & 154\,263 \\
End    & 16\,777\,216    & 109.01    & 153\,903 \\
Random & 6\,800\,733     & 43.00     & 154\,079 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The complete traversal of the reduced keyspace requires approximately 109 seconds, corresponding to a processing rate of approximately 153.9 thousand candidate keys per second.

The middle, end, and random scenarios exhibit similar candidate-processing rates, while the single-candidate start scenario is dominated by the extremely short measurement interval and should not be treated as a stable throughput estimate.

Only the complete-search scenario provides a direct empirical basis for a simple linear extrapolation under the assumption of constant candidate-processing rate.

However, such extrapolation remains specific to the measured software execution path. The result does not represent the exhaustive-search performance of DES, TDEA, GPUs, FPGA-based accelerators, ASIC implementations, or specialized cryptanalytic systems.

Moreover, fixed-key encryption throughput is not equivalent to candidate-key testing throughput. Exhaustive search requires additional work, particularly the generation or initialization of a candidate key and evaluation of a matching condition.

The distinction is fundamental because the two workloads differ in computational structure and measurement scope.

The present experiment therefore illustrates the limitations of interpreting encryption throughput as an exhaustive-search rate.

More advanced cryptanalytic techniques, including meet-in-the-middle attacks against multiple encryption constructions, require separate computational and memory-cost models and are outside the experimental scope of this study.

\subsubsection{Avalanche Effect}

The avalanche experiment examines the change in ciphertext output produced by modifying one bit of the plaintext or one effective bit of the cryptographic key.

For each tested pair, the Hamming distance between the original and modified ciphertext blocks is calculated.

The concept is related to the classical principle of diffusion in cryptographic systems, where changes in input information should influence multiple output components \cite{shannon1949communication}.

The Hamming distance is normalized by the block size: 64 bits for DES and TDEA, and 128 bits for AES.

Table~\ref{tab:avalanche} summarizes the observed median and mean Hamming distances for plaintext-bit (P) and effective key-bit (K) modifications.

\begin{table}[htbp]

          & Median norm. & Mean norm. \\
\midrule
DES     & P & 64  & 32 & 32.02 & 0.50 & 0.5003 \\
DES     & K & 64  & 32 & 31.94 & 0.50 & 0.4990 \\
TDEA    & P & 64  & 32 & 31.97 & 0.50 & 0.4995 \\
TDEA    & K & 64  & 32 & 32.02 & 0.50 & 0.5004 \\
AES-128 & P & 128 & 64 & 63.99 & 0.50 & 0.4999 \\
AES-128 & K & 128 & 64 & 63.99 & 0.50 & 0.4999 \\
AES-192 & P & 128 & 64 & 64.03 & 0.50 & 0.5002 \\
AES-192 & K & 128 & 64 & 64.09 & 0.50 & 0.5007 \\
AES-256 & P & 128 & 64 & 63.98 & 0.50 & 0.4998 \\
AES-256 & K & 128 & 64 & 64.00 & 0.50 & 0.5000 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

For DES and TDEA, the median Hamming distance is 32 out of 64 bits. For all AES variants, the median is 64 out of 128 bits.

Thus, the normalized median is 0.5 across all ten experimental groups.

The normalized mean values range from approximately 0.4990 to 0.5007, indicating that the observed output differences are concentrated around half of the corresponding ciphertext block size.

This behavior is consistent with the expected local diffusion properties of established block ciphers.

However, the result must not be interpreted as proof of cryptographic security or as evidence that the evaluated algorithms provide equivalent protection.

The avalanche experiment is not a complete evaluation of the Strict Avalanche Criterion (SAC), the Bit Independence Criterion (BIC), or resistance to differential, linear, or other forms of cryptanalysis.

Furthermore, normalized average Hamming distance alone is insufficient to characterize the full distribution of output changes or to distinguish securely designed ciphers from deliberately weakened constructions.

Accordingly, the avalanche results are treated as a descriptive demonstration rather than as a basis for ranking algorithmic security.
