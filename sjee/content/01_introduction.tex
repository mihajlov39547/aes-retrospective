
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The practical performance of cryptographic algorithms depends not only on their mathematical definitions but also on their implementation strategies, available hardware acceleration, and the methodology used to evaluate their execution costs. The same cryptographic primitive may exhibit substantially different performance characteristics when implemented in conventional CPU software, accelerated through dedicated processor instructions, or executed on a graphics processing unit (GPU). Consequently, comparisons based on a single throughput metric may be insufficient and potentially misleading unless the measurement scope and execution environment are explicitly defined.

The Data Encryption Standard (DES) and the Triple Data Encryption Algorithm (TDEA) remain relevant primarily from historical and methodological perspectives. The DES specification and its associated standards have been withdrawn, while TDEA is no longer approved by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) for applying new cryptographic protection \cite{nist1999des,nbs1980des,barker2017tdea,barker2019transitioning}. In contrast, the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) remains a widely adopted standard for symmetric-key encryption \cite{nist2023aes}. Nevertheless, meaningful comparisons between cryptographic algorithms require consideration of their key lengths, block sizes, modes of operation, security properties, and implementation characteristics. Modes such as Cipher Block Chaining (CBC), Counter (CTR), and Galois/Counter Mode (GCM) introduce different computational requirements and security properties, further complicating direct performance comparisons \cite{dworkin2001modes,dworkin2007gcm}.

GPU acceleration of AES has been investigated in several studies, demonstrating the potential of massively parallel architectures to achieve high encryption throughput \cite{manavski2007cuda,harrison2007aes,iwai2012acceleration,wang2019gpu,tezcan2021optimization}. However, the interpretation of GPU performance results depends critically on which operations are included in the measurement. In particular, throughput obtained from an isolated GPU kernel operating on device-resident data does not necessarily represent the performance of a complete application-level encryption pipeline. Host-to-device transfers, device-to-host transfers, kernel launches, synchronization, and runtime overhead may substantially affect the observed end-to-end execution time. These distinctions are consistent with established CUDA performance measurement and optimization practices \cite{nvidiaProgrammingGuide,nvidiaBestPractices,harris2012overlap}.

An additional challenge arises when GPU implementations are compared against CPU implementations that do not exploit available hardware acceleration. Modern x86 processors support dedicated AES instructions, commonly referred to as AES-NI, which significantly reduce the computational cost of AES operations compared with conventional software implementations \cite{intel2012aesni}. Therefore, comparing a CUDA implementation exclusively against a software-based CPU implementation may overstate the practical advantage of GPU acceleration. A more informative evaluation should distinguish between conventional CPU software execution, hardware-accelerated CPU execution, GPU kernel execution, and the complete host-driven GPU pipeline.

The central research problem addressed in this paper is how to establish a methodologically consistent, validated, and reproducible framework for evaluating cryptographic primitives across heterogeneous computing environments. Rather than introducing a new encryption algorithm, cryptanalytic technique, or state-of-the-art CUDA implementation, the study focuses on the experimental conditions under which performance comparisons can be considered meaningful. Particular attention is given to functional correctness, controlled input generation, consistent workload definitions, explicit measurement boundaries, and the preservation of experimental evidence.

The proposed methodology follows a validation-first benchmarking approach in which functional correctness is established before performance measurements are accepted. The experimental framework incorporates multiple execution profiles, including smoke, pilot, and study configurations, together with fixed random seeds, controlled input parameters, and paired comparisons. Measurements are organized into independent experimental sessions, while raw observations, aggregated summaries, validation results, and environment metadata are preserved to support reproducibility and subsequent analysis.

A central premise of the study is that performance claims must be interpreted within their specific measurement scopes. A CPU transform-only measurement, a GPU kernel-only measurement with device-resident data, and a complete GPU end-to-end measurement address different computational questions and should not be treated as interchangeable indicators of encryption performance. Accordingly, the experimental protocol distinguishes between conventional software AES, hardware-accelerated CPU AES-NI, CUDA resident-kernel execution, and host-driven CUDA end-to-end execution. This separation enables the computational benefits of GPU parallelism to be evaluated independently from the transfer and orchestration costs incurred by the complete processing pipeline.

The main methodological contribution is therefore the explicit separation of cryptographic correctness, computational throughput, and system-level execution costs within a reproducible experimental framework. The study further emphasizes three important interpretative distinctions: fixed-key GPU encryption throughput is not equivalent to exhaustive key-search performance; resident GPU throughput does not directly represent application-level throughput; and the avalanche effect, although useful for characterizing diffusion, does not constitute sufficient evidence of overall cryptographic security \cite{shannon1949communication,nist2023aes}. By maintaining these distinctions, the proposed framework aims to provide a more reliable basis for evaluating heterogeneous implementations of symmetric cryptographic primitives and interpreting the practical significance of CPU--GPU performance differences.
