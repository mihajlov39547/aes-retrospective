
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

More than two decades after the standardization of the
Advanced Encryption Standard (AES), the efficiency of
symmetric encryption remains an important consideration
in computing and communication systems. Although the
mathematical definition of AES has remained stable,
the hardware and software environments in which it
operates have changed substantially. Modern processors
provide dedicated cryptographic instructions, while
graphics processing units (GPUs) offer large numbers
of parallel execution resources that can be employed
for suitable cryptographic workloads. Consequently,
the practical performance of AES depends not only on
the algorithm itself, but also on its implementation,
the available acceleration mechanisms, data placement,
and the operations included in performance measurements.

The historical transition from the Data Encryption
Standard (DES), through the Triple Data Encryption
Algorithm (TDEA), to AES provides a useful perspective
on this development. DES and TDEA played important
roles in the evolution of symmetric cryptography,
but their limitations eventually led to the adoption
of stronger and more flexible alternatives. DES
standards have been withdrawn, while TDEA is no
longer approved by the National Institute of
Standards and Technology (NIST) for applying new
cryptographic protection
\cite{nist1999des,nbs1980des,barker2017tdea,
barker2019transitioning}.

AES, standardized in 2001 and subsequently updated
in FIPS~197, remains a fundamental symmetric-key
primitive~\cite{nist2023aes}. Its continued relevance
reflects not only its cryptographic design, but also
its adaptability to changing implementation environments.
The original transition from DES and TDEA to AES was
motivated primarily by security, efficiency, and
implementation requirements. Today, these considerations
extend to hardware-assisted execution, heterogeneous
computing, and the performance characteristics of
complete data-processing systems.

In telecommunications and networked environments,
the distinction between cryptographic primitives
and their application is especially important.
Encryption must coexist with requirements for
throughput, latency, authentication, and correct
management of cryptographic state. Cipher Block
Chaining (CBC), Counter (CTR), and Galois/Counter
Mode (GCM), for example, introduce different
computational dependencies and security properties
\cite{dworkin2001modes,dworkin2007gcm}.

Therefore, high throughput measured for an isolated
AES transformation cannot automatically be interpreted
as the performance of an authenticated network
protocol or a complete communication application.
The evaluated cryptographic functionality and
measurement boundary must match the intended
interpretation of the result.


\subsection{Motivation and Practical Relevance}
\label{subsec:introduction_motivation}

The increasing availability of GPU acceleration
raises a practical question for system designers:
can a GPU improve AES processing performance
when the host CPU already supports dedicated
cryptographic instructions?

This question is particularly relevant for
computers equipped with accessible NVIDIA GPUs,
including consumer and entry-level professional
laptop devices. Such hardware may offer substantial
parallel computational resources without requiring
a dedicated datacenter accelerator.

However, the availability of a GPU does not
automatically imply that transferring an
encryption workload to the accelerator will
reduce processing time.

Contemporary x86 processors commonly support
hardware-accelerated AES execution through
the Advanced Encryption Standard New Instructions
(AES-NI). These instructions substantially
reduce the computational cost of AES operations
relative to selected conventional software
implementations~\cite{intel2012aesni}.

A GPU that appears considerably faster than
software-only AES may therefore provide a
smaller advantage, or no advantage, when
compared against an AES-NI-enabled CPU path.

The choice of CPU reference is consequently
central to meaningful heterogeneous
performance evaluation.

A second consideration concerns the location
of the processed data.

A CUDA kernel can execute AES transformations
on buffers already resident in GPU memory.
However, applications that receive plaintext
in host memory and require ciphertext to be
returned to the host must also account for
the corresponding transfers and execution
orchestration.

The cost of these operations can materially
change the observed benefit of acceleration.

For applications that process large collections
of independent blocks or suitably organized
data batches, GPU execution may be attractive.
For small, latency-sensitive operations,
fixed dispatch, synchronization, and transfer
costs may instead favor direct CPU execution.

These observations motivate a benchmarking
approach that distinguishes computational
throughput from the performance of the
complete measured processing path.

The practical objective is not to recommend
GPU offloading for every encryption workload,
but to establish reproducible evidence
for determining when it may be advantageous.


\subsection{Background and Research Gap}
\label{subsec:introduction_gap}

GPU implementations of AES have been investigated
for many years, demonstrating the potential
of parallel hardware for accelerating block-cipher
operations
\cite{manavski2007cuda,harrison2007aes,
iwai2012acceleration,wang2019gpu,
tezcan2021optimization}.

These studies have examined implementation
strategies, parallel processing granularity,
memory organization, and techniques for
improving GPU execution efficiency.

For example, Iwai et al. investigated AES
acceleration using CUDA and demonstrated
the relevance of implementation granularity,
memory allocation, and overlapping data
transfers with computation
\cite{iwai2012acceleration}.

Consequently, the influence of data movement
on GPU encryption performance is not a
previously unexplored problem.

More broadly, Gregg and Hazelwood emphasized
that CPU--GPU comparisons can be misleading
when they omit the location of application
data and the costs of transferring data
between processing devices
\cite{gregg2011data}.

Lee et al. similarly demonstrated that
the apparent performance gap between CPUs
and GPUs depends strongly on the optimization
quality of the respective implementations
\cite{lee2010debunking}.

These contributions establish important
principles for heterogeneous performance
evaluation.

Nevertheless, they do not eliminate the need
to assess concrete cryptographic implementations
under reproducible and explicitly defined
measurement conditions.

A performance result obtained from isolated
GPU execution cannot be assumed to represent
a host-driven encryption pipeline.
Likewise, a speedup measured against conventional
software AES cannot automatically be transferred
to a comparison against hardware-assisted AES-NI.

Additional uncertainty arises when measurements
from different computing environments are
interpreted without preserving the relevant
hardware, software, workload, and statistical
context.

The research gap addressed in this paper is
therefore not the absence of previous GPU AES
implementations or prior awareness of
data-transfer overhead.

Rather, it concerns the need for an integrated,
auditable experimental assessment that combines
cryptographic validation, paired workloads,
a hardware-accelerated CPU reference,
explicit GPU timing boundaries, independent
study sessions, and source-level traceability
within a common analysis.

Such an assessment is particularly useful
when examining accessible laptop GPUs
alongside a datacenter GPU, because a
positive result on one system may not
generalize to another.

The study consequently emphasizes both
successful and unsuccessful offloading
conditions instead of selecting only
the highest observed throughput values.


\subsection{Research Objectives and Questions}
\label{subsec:introduction_questions}

The objective of this study is to establish
and apply a reproducible methodology for
evaluating the performance of AES
implementations across heterogeneous
CPU and GPU execution environments.

The analysis is motivated by the
historical evolution of symmetric
cryptography from DES and TDEA to AES,
but its principal experimental focus
is the contemporary relationship
between software CPU encryption,
hardware-assisted AES-NI, and
GPU-based AES execution.

The study addresses three research questions.

\begin{enumerate}

\item[\textbf{RQ1}]
Under which tested workload sizes,
AES modes, and implementation configurations
does GPU execution outperform conventional
software AES and hardware-assisted CPU AES-NI,
and which advantages remain consistent
across independent study sessions?

\item[\textbf{RQ2}]
How does expanding the GPU measurement
boundary from device-resident kernel execution
to a synchronous, transfer-inclusive
processing pipeline change the magnitude
and direction of the observed CPU--GPU
performance relationship?

\item[\textbf{RQ3}]
How do performance variability,
cipher-mode dependence, workload-size
effects, and differences among the
tested computing configurations
constrain the practical interpretation
and generalization of GPU acceleration?

\end{enumerate}

These questions distinguish the performance
of a cryptographic implementation from
the performance of a particular processing
workflow.

They also connect the experimental results
to practical architectural decisions:
whether an AES workload should remain
on a hardware-accelerated CPU or be
assigned to a GPU under a specified
set of execution conditions.


\subsection{Methodological Approach and Contributions}
\label{subsec:introduction_contributions}

The experimental methodology follows
a validation-first design in which
cryptographic correctness is established
before performance measurements are
accepted.

Deterministically generated inputs
and matching cryptographic parameters
support paired CPU--GPU comparisons,
while the execution paths are evaluated
using separately defined measurement
boundaries.

The framework distinguishes
conventional software AES,
hardware-accelerated CPU AES-NI,
device-resident CUDA kernel execution,
and a synchronous prepared-buffer
GPU pipeline including host--device
transfers and synchronization.

These measurements do not represent
interchangeable definitions of
encryption performance.

In particular, the prepared-buffer
pipeline includes a broader execution
scope than isolated kernel timing,
but does not include every operation
required by a complete encryption
application.

The principal experimental campaign
was conducted on three configurations:
a Windows~10 laptop with an NVIDIA
GeForce GTX~1650~Ti, a Windows~11
laptop with an NVIDIA T600 Laptop GPU,
and a Linux-based Google Colab
environment with an NVIDIA Tesla T4.

The first GPU represents a consumer
laptop device, the second an entry-level
professional mobile GPU, and the third
a datacenter-oriented accelerator.

Both laptop GPUs have 4~GB of device
memory, making the evaluation relevant
to relatively accessible GPU hardware.

The study comprises five sessions
per platform, producing fifteen
sessions and 18,000 recorded
timing observations.

The measurements cover AES-128
encryption in ECB and CTR modes,
five workload sizes, conventional
software and AES-NI CPU paths,
and baseline and optimized-v1
CUDA implementations.

The statistical analysis preserves
session-level pairing and reports
results with their observed variability.
Original timing records, validation
outcomes, environment metadata,
and implementation source snapshots
are retained for independent
inspection and reanalysis.

The paper makes three principal
contributions:

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{A validation-first,
reproducible benchmarking methodology.}

The study integrates functional
correctness checks, deterministic
paired workloads, explicit
measurement boundaries, session-based
aggregation, and archived
experimental evidence into a
coherent procedure for evaluating
cryptographic execution paths.

\item \textbf{A three-configuration
empirical assessment of AES acceleration.}

Fifteen study sessions and 18,000
raw timing observations characterize
software CPU AES, hardware-assisted
AES-NI, and two CUDA implementations
under resident and transfer-inclusive
GPU execution scopes.

Independent reconstruction of the
saved statistical estimators provides
additional evidence of the internal
consistency and auditability of
the reported results.

\item \textbf{An evidence-qualified
interpretation of GPU offloading.}

The analysis identifies conditions
under which accessible GPU hardware
can outperform a corresponding
AES-NI CPU reference, while also
documenting configurations in which
the advantage disappears or reverses.

Workload size, cipher mode,
CPU reference performance,
measurement scope, and
between-session variability
are treated as essential
qualifications of practical
acceleration claims.

\end{enumerate}

These contributions do not rely
on introducing a new AES algorithm,
a novel cryptanalytic method,
or a claim of record-setting
CUDA throughput.

Instead, the contribution lies
in applying a consistent and
verifiable experimental methodology
to practical heterogeneous
AES execution choices.


\subsection{Principal Findings and Significance}
\label{subsec:introduction_significance}

The results demonstrate why the
distinction between isolated
computation and transfer-inclusive
processing is practically important.

For 32~MiB AES-128 workloads,
device-resident GPU execution
outperforms the corresponding
host AES-NI reference in all
five sessions on each platform,
for both tested CUDA implementations
and both encryption modes.

However, this resident advantage
does not universally persist
when the synchronous GPU pipeline
includes data transfers and
synchronization.

A particularly relevant positive
result is obtained on the NVIDIA
T600 Laptop GPU.

For AES-128 CTR at 32~MiB,
its baseline CUDA pipeline
achieves a median paired speedup
of approximately $1.35\times$
relative to its host AES-NI path,
with the advantage observed
in all five sessions.

The Tesla T4 achieves a larger
paired CTR pipeline speedup
of approximately $4.76\times$
for the corresponding workload.

Conversely, the consumer
GTX~1650~Ti does not demonstrate
a consistently positive
pipeline advantage over AES-NI,
and the T4 ECB pipeline remains
slower than its own AES-NI
reference at 32~MiB.

These contrasting outcomes show
that GPU offloading can provide
a measurable benefit even when
transfers are included, but
that the benefit cannot be
inferred solely from GPU
kernel throughput or
hardware category.

The results also demonstrate
that relative GPU speedup
must be interpreted alongside
the performance of the
corresponding host CPU.

For system designers,
the implication is that
cryptographic acceleration
should be evaluated as a
workload-placement decision.

Large, parallelizable,
or device-resident
cryptographic workloads
may justify GPU evaluation,
whereas host-resident
or latency-sensitive
processing may favor
CPU AES-NI.

These observations are
relevant to heterogeneous
data-processing and
telecommunications systems,
although the present
measurements do not
constitute benchmarks of
complete TLS, IPsec,
or authenticated-encryption
applications.

The broader significance
is therefore methodological:
the observed advantage
of GPU acceleration depends
on what is measured, how
the workload is organized,
and which execution
path serves as the reference.


\subsection{Paper Organization}
\label{subsec:introduction_organization}

The remainder of the paper
is organized as follows.

Section~\ref{sec:methodology}
describes the experimental
platforms, correctness
validation, workload
definitions, paired
measurement protocol,
timing boundaries,
statistical aggregation,
and evidence-preservation
procedures.

Section~\ref{sec:results}
presents the retrospective
CPU experiments and
the principal heterogeneous
AES-128 results, including
absolute throughput,
paired speedup,
measurement-scope effects,
and session variability.

Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
interprets the findings
in relation to practical
GPU offloading,
CPU hardware acceleration,
data placement,
telecommunications
applications, and
limitations of the
tested configurations.

Finally,
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}
summarizes the empirical
findings and the
methodological contribution
to reproducible
cryptographic performance
evaluation.
