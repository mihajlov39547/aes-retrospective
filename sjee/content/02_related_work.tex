\section{Related Work and Research Gap}
\label{sec:related_work}

Early contributions by Manavski~\cite{manavski2007cuda} and Harrison and
Waldron~\cite{harrison2007aes} established the feasibility of AES execution on programmable GPU
architectures. Subsequent work investigated how parallel processing, memory organization, and
architecture-specific optimization affect encryption throughput. The available parallelism
depends partly on the encryption mode: ECB permits independent block transformations, while CTR
generates keystream from distinct counter values~\cite{dworkin2001modes}. Both expose
independent work for GPU threads when buffers are sufficiently large. ECB is unsuitable for
protecting general structured data and serves here only as a controlled performance reference.

Iwai et al.~\cite{iwai2012acceleration} examined thread granularity, memory organization, and
the interaction between computation and data transfers, including overlapping transfers with
computation. Their CUDA implementation achieved approximately 35~Gbit/s for device-side AES
processing and approximately 22.5~Gbit/s for transfer-aware execution. These results demonstrate
that the execution boundary and transfer strategy affect the reported encryption rate. Wang and
Chu~\cite{wang2019gpu} investigated lookup-table-based AES implementations and GPU execution
strategies. Tezcan~\cite{tezcan2021optimization} examined shared-memory optimization and
bank-conflict avoidance, reporting AES-128 encryption throughput of 878.6~Gbit/s on an NVIDIA
RTX~2070~Super.

More recent work has explored bit-sliced cipher representations. A 2024
study~\cite{bitsliced2024gpu} reported high-performance AES-CTR and AES-ECB execution on desktop
and embedded NVIDIA platforms. Together, these studies demonstrate the computational
capabilities of optimized GPU implementations. Their reported rates must nevertheless be
interpreted within their respective measurement boundaries: device-side encryption throughput
does not directly specify the rate at which an application can receive, encrypt, and deliver
data. It also does not establish whether offloading improves performance relative to the host
processor available in a particular system.

That comparison depends on the CPU implementation. Dedicated instructions such as AES-NI reduce
the cost of AES transformations~\cite{intel2012aesni} and provide a hardware-assisted baseline
for evaluating GPU offloading. Lee et al.~\cite{lee2010debunking} demonstrated that conclusions
about heterogeneous acceleration depend strongly on the optimization level of the
implementations being evaluated. Their findings support using an appropriately accelerated CPU
reference when deciding whether GPU offloading offers an additional processing benefit.
Accordingly, comparing GPU encryption with conventional unaccelerated software cannot establish
an advantage over the host's hardware-assisted AES path. The reference must represent the
execution path against which an offloading decision will actually be made, and the comparison
remains specific to the implementations evaluated.

Data location further determines which costs enter that decision. Gregg and
Hazelwood~\cite{gregg2011data} showed that CPU--GPU performance evaluation requires explicit
consideration of data movement. An advantage for device-resident input may shrink when both
input and output cross the host--device boundary. CUDA guidance recommends minimizing
unnecessary transfers, using appropriate synchronization, and considering overlap between
communication and computation~\cite{nvidiaBestPractices,harris2012overlap}. The transfer-aware
AES work of Iwai et al.~\cite{iwai2012acceleration} demonstrates that this concern is already
established in GPU cryptographic processing.

For communication infrastructure, these findings connect encryption performance to the
surrounding processing path. A host-managed interface may receive data, organize buffers, invoke
encryption, and forward the output. The resulting costs differ between contiguous buffers,
aggregated independent messages, and individual latency-sensitive packets. Device-resident
measurements and transfer-inclusive measurements therefore answer different questions. The
present study distinguishes resident execution from a synchronous prepared-buffer pipeline
containing host-to-device and device-to-host transfers. This pipeline measures a broader
operation than the kernel alone, but excludes complete network input/output and application
processing; it also does not evaluate transfer--computation overlap.

Telecommunications security adds requirements beyond encryption throughput. AES-GCM combines
counter-mode encryption with GHASH authentication~\cite{dworkin2007gcm}; an improvement in
AES-CTR can therefore concern one component of an authenticated-encryption pipeline without
establishing an equivalent improvement in IPsec or TLS processing. Practical implementations
must also account for nonce management, packet preparation, synchronization, and output
delivery. These requirements distinguish a bulk encryption benchmark from a complete security
service, even when the underlying cipher operations are parallelizable.

Earlier research also examined GPU-assisted encryption in communication protocols.
Sch{\"o}nberger and Fu{\ss}~\cite{schonberger2011gpu} investigated GPU precomputation of
AES counter-mode keystream for an IPsec-oriented GCM implementation, with subsequent encryption
and authentication performed on the CPU. More recently, Lee et al.~\cite{lee2025parallelgcm}
developed a parallel GPU implementation of GCM, including GHASH processing, and reported
throughput exceeding 400~Gbit/s on an RTX~4090. Thus, GPU-assisted telecommunications encryption
and authenticated-encryption acceleration are established research directions. The present
study instead characterizes reproducible operating conditions for bulk AES-128 processing,
distinguishing device-resident execution from synchronous transfer-inclusive processing against
hardware-assisted AES-NI. It neither introduces a new AES algorithm nor benchmarks complete
AES-GCM or IPsec processing.

Workload aggregation introduces a further throughput--latency trade-off. Combining small
messages may increase the work available to the GPU, while waiting for a batch adds queueing
delay. Bulk data protection, traffic aggregation, heterogeneous communication gateways, and
data-processing services are consequently possible application contexts, but their suitability
depends on host acceleration, data placement, and operational requirements. Peak kernel rates
alone cannot determine that suitability. The present measurements characterize contiguous bulk
buffers and do not demonstrate packet batching or complete telecommunications protocol
performance.

Assessing whether an observed advantage is repeatable also requires control of experimental
variability. Mytkowicz et al.~\cite{mytkowicz2009wrong} showed that experimental conditions can
introduce systematic bias and alter performance results. Kalibera and
Jones~\cite{kalibera2013rigorous} emphasized the importance of accounting for variability across
multiple levels of experimental replication, while Hoefler and
Belli~\cite{hoefler2015scientific} discussed scientifically defensible performance evaluation of
parallel computing systems. These contributions provide a methodological basis for determining
whether an acceleration is repeatable under the conditions tested. These studies motivate
reporting repeated observations and their execution context rather than relying on the highest
throughput measurement. For cryptographic workloads, functional correctness must additionally be
verified before timings are accepted as evidence of encryption performance.

The present evaluation applies these principles through cryptographic validation, paired
workloads, repeated study sessions, documented timing boundaries, and preservation of raw
observations and experimental provenance. Session-level evidence distinguishes isolated
favorable measurements from advantages observed consistently across executions, while retaining
outcomes that favor the CPU. Detailed procedures and aggregation equations are given in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology}.

The literature thus establishes GPU AES feasibility, optimization techniques, and the need to
account for transfers, CPU acceleration, and measurement variability. The question addressed
here is which operating conditions produce a repeatable bulk-processing benefit when the host
already supports hardware-assisted encryption. High kernel throughput alone cannot answer this
question, and an unfavorable transfer-inclusive result on one system cannot settle it for other
platforms or workloads.

To examine these conditions, the study compares AES-128 ECB and CTR workloads of different sizes
across three heterogeneous configurations under a common validation and measurement protocol.
The comparison uses hardware-assisted CPU AES-NI as its reference and retains session-level
measurements for assessing consistency across repeated executions. Its contribution is the
identification of experimentally supported operating conditions in which GPU-assisted bulk AES
processing provides an advantage, together with evidence of constraints under which that
advantage disappears or becomes inconsistent. This supports assessment of GPU-assisted bulk
encryption and workload placement within the tested execution boundaries. Extending those
findings to authenticated encryption and network processing requires separate application-level
measurements.
