\section{Introduction}
The Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) is widely used to protect stored and
transmitted data~\cite{nist2023aes}. Counter (CTR) mode exposes independent
block-encryption operations and is amenable to parallel execution
~\cite{dworkin2001modes}. Nevertheless, a GPU is not automatically the fastest
place to perform encryption: host processors provide dedicated AES instructions,
and moving plaintext and ciphertext across the CPU--GPU boundary consumes time.
An application using an accelerator must therefore distinguish the cost of AES
computation from the cost of placing data where that computation occurs.

This distinction matters for storage and analytics workloads consisting of many
independent data units. The Apache Parquet encryption specification provides a
concrete motivation: its \texttt{AES\_GCM\_CTR\_V1} mode uses CTR encryption for
data pages and GCM protection for other encrypted modules. Rather than claim
support for a complete Parquet file, we study a controlled, Parquet-inspired
workload of independent 1~MiB pages with a distinct 96-bit nonce and a 32-bit
counter initialized to one for each logical page. The study does not implement
file framing, authenticated metadata, key management, compression, or Parquet
interoperability. CTR encryption alone does not provide authentication.

The central question is not simply whether a GPU can execute AES quickly, but
\emph{where a measured advantage exists and what must change in data placement
for an application to exploit it}. A GPU-resident input can be encrypted without
round-trip payload movement. A CPU-resident input that must return to host
memory after GPU encryption requires two transfers. These are distinct
workflows and must not be described by a single generic ``GPU speedup.''

The study makes three bounded contributions. First, it establishes a
same-runtime comparison with native OpenSSL EVP AES-NI, correcting the
interpretation of earlier comparisons that used PyCryptodome as their host
reference. Second, it quantifies how a GPU compute-only advantage for 32~MiB
CTR is lost at a prepared transfer-inclusive boundary. Third, it validates
independent nonce/counter domains per page and shows that their GPU processing
cost is close to the cost of a continuous stream of equal length. The
contribution is a measured execution-placement distinction and a reproducible
page-aware implementation, not a new AES primitive or a demonstrated complete
storage-engine speedup.

Previously recorded measurements on GTX 1650 Ti, T600 and Tesla T4 are retained
as historical, implementation-specific evidence. New same-runtime T4 CPU/GPU
and page-batching sessions are presented separately; their observations are
not pooled with the earlier data. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} defines the
measurement boundaries, Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the positive and
negative results together, and Sections~\ref{sec:discussion} and
\ref{sec:conclusion} discuss the practical conditions and remaining work.
