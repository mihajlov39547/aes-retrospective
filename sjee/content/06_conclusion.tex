
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

More than two decades after the standardization of the
Advanced Encryption Standard (AES), the evolution of
computing architectures continues to influence how
efficiently symmetric encryption can be implemented
and deployed. Building on a retrospective examination
of the transition from DES and Triple DES to AES,
this study investigated the practical performance
of modern CPU and GPU cryptographic implementations,
with particular emphasis on the conditions under
which GPU acceleration can outperform CPUs equipped
with dedicated AES instructions.

The principal contribution of this work is a
validation-first, reproducible benchmarking
methodology that integrates cryptographic correctness
verification, deterministic and paired workloads,
explicitly defined measurement boundaries,
session-based statistical aggregation, and preservation
of the experimental evidence required for independent
analysis. Rather than presenting encryption throughput
as an implementation-independent characteristic of AES,
the framework distinguishes the performance of
software CPU execution, hardware-assisted AES-NI,
device-resident CUDA computation, and a synchronous
GPU pipeline that includes host--device transfers
and synchronization.

The methodology was evaluated across three
heterogeneous computing configurations: a Windows~10
system equipped with a consumer NVIDIA GeForce
GTX~1650~Ti, a Windows~11 system equipped with an
NVIDIA T600 Laptop GPU, and a Linux-based Google
Colab environment using an NVIDIA Tesla T4.
The two laptop GPUs each provide 4~GB of device
memory, demonstrating the relevance of the study
to accessible computing hardware as well as
datacenter-oriented acceleration.

The experimental campaign comprised fifteen study
sessions and 18,000 raw timing observations.
Cryptographic validation preceded performance
measurement, while an independent analysis
reconstructed the saved statistical estimators
without detecting numerical discrepancies within
the specified tolerances. These procedures provide
traceability between the measured implementations,
their execution environments, and the reported
performance conclusions.

The results demonstrate that GPU acceleration can
outperform even a hardware-accelerated CPU AES-NI
implementation under selected operating conditions.
For AES-128 CTR encryption of a 32~MiB workload,
the baseline CUDA implementation on the T600 Laptop
GPU achieved a median paired speedup of
$1.354\times$ over its host CPU AES-NI execution
path, with an advantage observed in all five
sessions. This corresponds to approximately
26\% lower paired processing latency within
the defined prepared-buffer GPU pipeline.

The Tesla T4 achieved a substantially larger
$4.761\times$ paired pipeline speedup for the
same AES mode and workload size, also outperforming
its host AES-NI reference in all five sessions.
These findings establish that a transfer-inclusive
GPU execution path can provide an advantage over
CPU hardware-assisted encryption, including on
a comparatively accessible 4~GB laptop GPU.

However, the experiments also demonstrate why
this advantage cannot be generalized without
considering the measurement boundary and CPU
reference. On the consumer GTX~1650~Ti, the
32~MiB baseline CTR pipeline achieved a
$4.566\times$ speedup over conventional software
AES but only $0.935\times$ relative to AES-NI.
The T4 likewise exhibited a mode-dependent
contrast: despite similar absolute GPU throughput
for ECB and CTR at 32~MiB, its ECB pipeline
achieved only $0.893\times$ the corresponding
host AES-NI performance.

Across all three platforms, device-resident
CUDA execution outperformed the paired AES-NI
reference at 32~MiB for both tested CUDA
implementations and both encryption modes,
with the advantage observed in all five
sessions per configuration. Nevertheless,
the same consistency did not extend to
transfer-inclusive pipeline execution.
The lightweight optimized-v1 CUDA candidate
also failed to provide uniform improvements
across platforms, workload sizes, cipher
modes, and measurement scopes.

Together, these results demonstrate that
high isolated kernel throughput is neither
a sufficient condition nor a reliable proxy
for advantageous GPU offloading. The observed
performance relationship depends on the
location of data, the costs of host--device
movement, the selected CPU implementation,
and the processing operations included
within the timed interval.

For telecommunications and heterogeneous
data-processing systems, the findings have
a practical architectural implication.
CPU AES-NI remains a strong reference for
small, latency-sensitive, or host-resident
workloads, whereas GPU processing warrants
evaluation for suitable large, parallelizable,
or device-resident operations. The observed
32~MiB CTR advantages indicate that GPU
acceleration may be valuable for bulk
cryptographic processing and appropriately
batched workloads, rather than for every
individual communication packet.

These observations support workload-aware
selection of cryptographic execution paths
instead of a universal preference for either
CPU or GPU execution. Nevertheless, the
present experiments do not benchmark complete
TLS, IPsec, or application-level authenticated
encryption. Their results therefore provide
evidence for architectural assessment rather
than a direct deployment recommendation
for secure communication protocols.

The retrospective DES/TDEA comparisons,
CPU AES-GCM measurements, reduced-keyspace
search, and avalanche experiments provide
complementary perspectives on the evolution
and evaluation of symmetric cryptography.
They also reinforce the need to distinguish
cryptographic functionality, encryption
throughput, exhaustive-search performance,
and security-related statistical properties.
These metrics characterize different
questions and should not be treated as
interchangeable evidence.

The principal limitations arise from the
evaluation of one system or cloud allocation
per platform, five study sessions per
configuration, selected library and CUDA
implementations, and a synchronous
prepared-buffer GPU pipeline. Differences
among the tested hardware and software
environments prevent causal attribution
to operating systems, GPU product classes,
or individual runtime components.
Furthermore, the tested ECB and CTR
workloads do not establish performance
for complete authenticated-encryption
or network-processing systems.

Future research may extend the methodology
to application-level AES-GCM evaluation,
network-packet batching, asynchronous
transfer--compute pipelines, workload-aware
CPU--GPU scheduling, and newer architectures
with dedicated cryptographic acceleration.
Such studies could investigate whether
the performance advantages observed for
large independent AES workloads remain
beneficial when authentication, communication
latency, resource contention, and complete
application data paths are considered.

In conclusion, this study establishes that
GPU acceleration is a viable but conditional
alternative to hardware-assisted CPU AES
execution. Its significance lies not in
claiming a universally faster implementation,
but in demonstrating reproducible conditions
under which GPU offloading succeeds or fails.
By combining validated cryptographic execution,
explicit measurement boundaries, and
independently auditable experimental results,
the proposed methodology provides a practical
foundation for evaluating cryptographic
acceleration and informing the design of
future heterogeneous computing and
communication systems.
